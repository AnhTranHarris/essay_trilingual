# Essay Structure and Psychology Lens

## Status

**FOUNDATION ONLY. This file defines the analytical architecture; it contains no essay draft.**

## Mission of the introduction

The introduction must explicitly state that the paper's primary mission is an **analysis of metaphors in F. Scott Fitzgerald's English-language *The Great Gatsby*.**

It must also explain, briefly and without distracting from the literary analysis, that the completed English essay is designed to be translated into Spanish and Mandarin/Simplified Chinese for readability and to satisfy the application requirement.

The translation requirement is a production and accessibility layer. It is not the primary literary subject of the paper.

## Planned analytical structure

The English paper will contain:

1. Introduction / mission / thesis.
2. Seven body sections centered on seven verified metaphorical constructions.
3. Conclusion.
4. Work(s) Cited on a separate page.

The body should progress as a coherent argument rather than seven isolated mini-essays.

## Seven candidate metaphorical constructions

These are **candidate anchors**. They are not approved quotations until the evidence ledger verifies exact wording, chapter, printed page, device classification, and context against the 1925 first edition.

### M1 — Moral foundations: hard rock / wet marshes
Nick's opening distinction between conduct founded on "hard rock" or "wet marshes."

Purpose:
- establish the novel's moral vocabulary;
- introduce instability versus solidity;
- provide a baseline against which later characters and choices can be evaluated.

Psychology use:
- minimal; literary and ethical analysis should dominate.

### M2 — Corruption trailing aspiration: foul dust in the wake of dreams
Nick's image of "foul dust" floating in the wake of Gatsby's dreams.

Purpose:
- separate Gatsby's capacity for hope from the forces surrounding and contaminating it;
- connect aspiration to residue, pursuit, and aftermath.

Psychology use:
- Freud may be used cautiously to distinguish desire from the conditions that frustrate or deform it.

### M3 — The valley of ashes as a fantastic farm
The Valley of Ashes is described as a "fantastic farm" where ashes grow "like wheat."

Purpose:
- analyze the inversion of fertility into waste;
- connect material production, class, and human depletion;
- explicitly distinguish the extended metaphor from the simile embedded within it.

Psychology use:
- modern social expectations and status may be discussed only where they illuminate the human meaning of the image.

### M4 — Gatsby as a self-created figure: Platonic conception / son of God
Nick describes Gatsby as springing from his "Platonic conception of himself" and extends the image through religious self-creation.

Purpose:
- analyze identity as deliberate invention;
- distinguish James Gatz from the idealized Jay Gatsby;
- examine the costs of building the self around an imagined ideal.

Psychology use:
- Freud may supply a historical lens for idealization and the relation between the self and the desired ideal.
- modern masculinity material may illuminate social pressure, status, and male self-definition without diagnosing Gatsby.

### M5 — Daisy's voice is full of money
Gatsby's direct metaphor identifies Daisy's voice with money.

Purpose:
- connect romantic attraction, class, security, status, and inherited privilege;
- show how emotional desire becomes inseparable from material aspiration.

Psychology use:
- Freud's discussion of desire, love, and social value may be used cautiously.
- SAMHSA's discussion of masculinity ideologies and social expectations may provide a modern comparative lens for status-oriented male identity.

### M6 — The past as something physically lurking and nearly reachable
Gatsby behaves as though the past is physically present, "lurking" near his house and just beyond reach.

Purpose:
- analyze time as an object Gatsby believes can be recovered;
- show how memory becomes spatialized and actionable;
- connect desire to attempted repetition.

Psychology use:
- *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* may be used as a historical interpretive lens for repetition and return.
- do not diagnose Gatsby with a disorder.

### M7 — Boats against the current
The closing extended metaphor presents human beings as boats moving forward while being carried backward toward the past.

Purpose:
- synthesize time, aspiration, repetition, loss, and human limitation;
- connect the novel's final image back to earlier metaphors;
- provide the vertical bridge into the conclusion.

Psychology use:
- Freud's repetition framework may be compared with Fitzgerald's image, but Fitzgerald remains the authority.
- the section should remain literary rather than clinical.

## The green light

The green light is central to the novel but is more accurately classified as a **symbol** than a strict metaphor.

It may appear as supporting evidence in sections M5, M6, or M7, especially when discussing projected desire and the receding future.

Do not falsely label it a simple metaphor merely because it is famous.

This distinction is intentional evidence of literary precision.

## Psychological-source hierarchy

Psychology is a supporting lens, not the paper's governing subject.

### Primary authority
Fitzgerald's English-language *The Great Gatsby*.

### Historical interpretive lens
Sigmund Freud:
- *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*
- *Civilization and Its Discontents*

### Modern male-psychology / behavioral-health lens
SAMHSA:
- *Addressing the Specific Behavioral Health Needs of Men*, TIP 56.

## Psychology guardrails

The essay must not:
- diagnose Gatsby, Tom, Nick, Daisy, or any fictional character;
- claim Freud explains Fitzgerald's intent without historical evidence;
- treat early psychoanalysis as current settled science;
- force every metaphor into a psychological theory;
- let secondary psychology sources outnumber or overshadow Fitzgerald evidence;
- use psychological jargon where plain language would be clearer.

The essay may:
- use Freud as a historically influential interpretive framework;
- compare Gatsby's repeated return to the past with Freud's concept of repetition;
- use modern male-psychology material to discuss social expectations, status, emotional restraint, and masculine role construction;
- identify where older and modern frameworks disagree or operate at different levels.

## Vertical-argument requirement

The seven metaphor sections must form one developing line of reasoning.

Provisional vertical progression:

**moral framework -> corrupted aspiration -> social waste -> self-invention -> desire fused with class -> attempted recovery of the past -> universal struggle against time**

The final thesis must be broad enough to contain this progression but narrow enough that every body section materially supports it.

## Translation requirement

Spanish and Mandarin/Simplified-Chinese versions are produced only after the English analytical version is frozen.

Psychological distinctions, levels of certainty, and metaphor classifications must survive translation.

## Drafting gate

No prose drafting begins until the seven candidate anchors are entered into the evidence ledger and verified against the 1925 first-edition page images.
