# Spanish Translation Style Guide

## Status

**LOCKED TRANSLATION STANDARD**

The Spanish version must read as high-quality, educated, formal Spanish centered on **Mexico City / standard Mexican usage**, while remaining comfortable and intelligible for readers across Latin America and international Spanish-speaking audiences.

This is not a literal translation exercise.

## Target variety

Use:

**formal, educated Mexican Spanish with international readability**

The target should sound natural to an educated reader in Mexico City without becoming highly regional, colloquial, or slang-heavy.

Avoid:
- Spain-specific `vosotros` forms;
- strongly Iberian lexical choices when a broadly understood Latin American equivalent is available;
- Mexican slang, regionalisms, jokes, or idioms that would narrow international readability;
- English syntactic calques;
- word-for-word translation that preserves English sentence structure unnaturally;
- inflated bureaucratic Spanish;
- unnecessary anglicisms.

Prefer:
- `ustedes` rather than `vosotros`;
- standard academic vocabulary used naturally in Mexico;
- clear Latin American punctuation and sentence rhythm;
- vocabulary that remains intelligible in Mexico, the United States, and most of Latin America;
- natural restructuring of long English sentences when Spanish requires it.

## Academic register

The Spanish essay should preserve the English paper's target level:

**strong AP-level analytical clarity + early college literary sophistication**

It should not sound:
- like a machine translation;
- like a legal document;
- like a graduate literary-theory paper;
- like informal conversation.

It should sound like polished academic Spanish written for an educated general reader.

## Canonical relationship to English

The frozen English essay is authoritative.

Spanish may alter:
- syntax;
- sentence order within a paragraph;
- idiom;
- punctuation;
- lexical choice;
- rhetorical rhythm.

Spanish may not alter:
- thesis;
- factual claims;
- Fitzgerald evidence;
- metaphor classification;
- degree of certainty;
- intentional ambiguity;
- psychological-source limitations;
- argumentative dependencies;
- conclusion.

## Literary terminology

Maintain consistent terminology throughout the paper.

Default controlled terms:

- metaphor -> `metáfora`
- simile -> `símil`
- symbol -> `símbolo`
- symbolism -> `simbolismo`
- imagery -> `imaginería` only where natural; otherwise use `imágenes` or `lenguaje figurado` according to context
- personification -> `personificación`
- allusion -> `alusión`
- narrator -> `narrador`
- narrative tension -> `tensión narrativa`
- ambiguity -> `ambigüedad`
- aspiration -> `aspiración`
- self-invention -> `autoinvención` or a natural contextual phrase such as `construcción de sí mismo`; once selected for a section, remain consistent
- social class -> `clase social`
- inherited wealth -> `riqueza heredada`
- memory -> `memoria`
- the past -> `el pasado`
- desire -> `deseo`
- moral judgment -> `juicio moral`
- social decay -> `decadencia social`
- repetition compulsion -> `compulsión de repetición`

If a controlled term sounds unnatural in a particular sentence, preserve the concept rather than forcing the glossary mechanically, but record the deviation in QA.

## Fitzgerald quotations

The English 1925 novel remains the literary authority.

For each translated Fitzgerald quotation:
1. map the quotation to its approved English evidence ID;
2. translate from the verified English quotation;
3. preserve the metaphorical relationship;
4. do not imply the wording comes from a published Spanish edition;
5. identify it as a project/authors' translation where MLA practice requires;
6. preserve the original Fitzgerald page number because the citation points to the English edition actually used.

Where useful for transparency, the first relevant note may explain that Spanish renderings of Fitzgerald quotations are translations by the authors from the cited 1925 English edition.

## Mexico City quality review

Every translated paragraph must pass four Spanish-specific checks.

### 1. Native-flow check
Read the Spanish without looking at the English.

Question:
Would this paragraph sound naturally written in formal Mexican Spanish?

Flag:
- English word order;
- excessive passive voice;
- unnatural noun stacking;
- awkward literal idioms;
- overuse of gerunds;
- unnecessary subject pronouns;
- repeated connectors.

### 2. International-readability check
Ask whether a professional reader from outside Mexico would understand the paragraph without needing regional knowledge.

Replace unnecessary:
- slang;
- local idioms;
- culturally narrow expressions.

Keep Mexican usage neutral rather than erasing it into artificial "global Spanish."

### 3. Semantic-equivalence check
Compare against the frozen English paragraph.

Verify:
- same proposition;
- same qualification;
- same evidence;
- same certainty;
- same contradiction or tension;
- same rhetorical purpose.

### 4. Literary-register check
Verify that the prose remains readable, analytical, and elegant.

Do not improve the translation by making it more abstract than the English original.

## Back-translation QA

After a Spanish paragraph is drafted, perform an independent meaning-focused back-translation into English.

The back-translation does not need matching wording.

It must preserve:
- argument;
- causal relationships;
- metaphorical relationships;
- uncertainty;
- source attribution;
- intentional contradiction.

Material drift requires revision.

## Vertical Spanish audit

After paragraph-level QA, read the complete Spanish essay independently.

Check:
- thesis stability;
- terminology consistency;
- recurring images;
- pronoun/reference clarity;
- transitions;
- contradiction handling;
- conclusion alignment;
- consistency of academic register.

A paragraph may be individually accurate and still fail because the complete Spanish paper sounds fragmented or translated. Both local and global quality must pass.

## AI-quality rule

The Spanish must not advertise AI through poor habits.

Avoid:
- repetitive `En este sentido`, `Por lo tanto`, `Cabe destacar`, or similar formulaic transitions;
- unnecessary synonyms that create terminology drift;
- excessive nominalization;
- overly symmetrical sentence patterns;
- exaggerated academic vocabulary.

The desired result is polished Mexican academic Spanish whose AI-assisted production is visible through consistency and quality control, not machine-like phrasing.

## Final gate

The Spanish version cannot be frozen until it passes:

**Mexico City native-flow QA + international-readability QA + semantic back-check + citation QA + horizontal coherence + vertical coherence.**
