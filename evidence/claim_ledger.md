## Central thesis

### CLM-001 — Governing thesis

Fitzgerald's metaphors make invisible pressures—judgment, aspiration, social class, identity, desire, memory, and time—material enough to see, hear, or physically imagine. Across the novel, those images move from unstable moral ground toward the final current of time, showing that Gatsby's unusual power to imagine life otherwise is both his defining strength and the source of his vulnerability when money, social structure, and time refuse to yield to reinvention.

Evidence dependencies:
- GG-M-001 through GG-M-007

Status: PROVISIONAL-APPROVED

## Section claims

### CLM-010 — Moral ground
Nick's hard-rock / wet-marsh metaphor establishes moral stability as a physical foundation while also exposing a productive contradiction in Nick himself: he claims tolerance yet narrates from a position of judgment.

Evidence:
- GG-M-001

Relationship:
- establishes the evaluative baseline for the rest of the essay.

Potential contradiction:
- Nick presents himself as tolerant while later judging nearly every major character.

Resolution:
- treat this as deliberate narrative tension, not an error; his claimed tolerance and actual judgment coexist and make his narration more complex.

### CLM-020 — Foul dust
The "foul dust" image separates Gatsby's capacity for hope from the corruption that follows and contaminates that hope.

Evidence:
- GG-M-002

Relationship:
- develops CLM-010 by moving from moral foundation to the corruption that gathers around aspiration.

### CLM-030 — Fantastic farm
The Valley of Ashes reverses the imagery of agricultural growth: instead of producing food or life, the landscape grows residue and human exhaustion.

Evidence:
- GG-M-003

Relationship:
- moves the argument from private aspiration into the social landscape that materially supports wealth.

### CLM-040 — Self-invention
Gatsby's "Platonic conception" transforms identity into an act of self-design. The metaphor explains both his extraordinary discipline and the danger of treating an ideal self as more authoritative than lived reality.

Evidence:
- GG-M-004

Secondary lens:
- Freud may be used cautiously if a verified concept helps explain idealization or the relation between the self and a desired ideal.
- SAMHSA may be used selectively for modern social expectations of masculine success and self-sufficiency.

### CLM-050 — Money in Daisy's voice
The money metaphor reveals that Gatsby's romantic desire and his attraction to class privilege have become psychologically and socially entangled.

Evidence:
- GG-M-005

Secondary lens:
- Freud's discussion of success, riches, love, and human standards may be useful.
- SAMHSA's discussion of masculine social expectations may be useful if it clarifies Gatsby's status-oriented self-construction.

Qualification:
- do not reduce Daisy to money alone; the claim concerns Gatsby's perception and the social meaning carried by her voice.

### CLM-060 — The recoverable past
The spatial image of the past "lurking" within reach shows Gatsby treating time as if it were an object that determination could recover.

Evidence:
- GG-M-006

Secondary lens:
- *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* may be used as a historical interpretive comparison for repetition and attempted return.

Guardrail:
- do not clinically diagnose Gatsby or claim Freud explains Fitzgerald's intent.

### CLM-070 — Boats against the current
The closing boat metaphor expands Gatsby's private struggle into a broader human condition: people act toward the future while memory, history, and prior attachment exert backward pressure.

Evidence:
- GG-M-007

Relationship:
- synthesizes CLM-010 through CLM-060 and returns the paper to the physical-language pattern established by the opening "ground" metaphor.

## Conclusion claim

### CLM-090 — Synthesis

The seven metaphors form a vertical progression:

moral ground -> contaminated aspiration -> social waste -> invented identity -> desire fused with class -> attempted recovery of the past -> human movement against time.

The conclusion should show that Fitzgerald's figurative language does more than decorate the novel: it provides a physical vocabulary for forces that the characters cannot otherwise hold still.

Status: PROVISIONAL-APPROVED
